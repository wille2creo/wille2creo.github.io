### Making Money

Perhaps gains, losses, and choices weigh so heavily because they draw on more than money. They point to outcomes that never came to pass: the mistake not made, the rally not missed, the career in which I did not remain where I was; and behind them, the self who chose otherwise at some fork in the road and had another life within reach.

Why does money lend itself so readily to these unlived lives? I find myself wondering, too, when it began to matter so much. I used to think I had cared little for it when young, and only after some years of work grew eager to earn more. Looking back, that account is not quite true. I wanted to make money early on; the desire merely wore different faces. An opportunity made it vivid. A setback drove it under, leaving me to suppose I had never cared much about money at all. Then another opportunity appeared, and the desire returned.

What passed for indifference, then, may not have been detachment. It may have been desire lying low after a defeat.

I did once imagine a life of modest means. I did not, I now see, want poverty itself, or its consequences for housing, health care, aging, and family responsibility. I wanted a certain image of life: money at a distance, a reasonable income sufficient for daily expenses, and my attention free for reading, writing, travel, play—for living. There was probably some vanity in it, the idea that a person less intimate with money would thereby be more free.

By now it is worth saying plainly: money matters. It affords security, choice, the means to leave. With more capital behind me, I could face an unwelcome job with greater composure; I could bear the cost of resting, changing direction, or declining an arrangement I did not wish to accept. Adult freedom has material conditions. Without the means, “I can leave whenever I like” is often no more than a sentence.

Nor do I wish to dismiss the desire to earn as a base impulse to be overcome. I want more money; the increase itself gives me pleasure. The difficulty begins where money takes on work beyond sustaining a life. Most conspicuously, the work of comparison.

### Comparison

We seldom measure ourselves against everyone. Those who have attained extraordinary wealth or professional success rarely make me feel a failure; they stand too far away to serve as a measure. The comparisons that touch me are with people near enough: classmates, colleagues in similar circumstances, friends whose ages and careers resemble mine. Having once stood so close, we seem to have diverged by a distance that can be reckoned.

I do not know their incomes, spending, or investments, but comparison has never required a complete set of accounts. A stranger's fortune would probably strike me as a mixture of ability and luck. But when someone who once worked beside me travels a long way from that shared starting point following a change of career, the thought comes readily: why did I not go?

Today's successful companies did not print the outcome on their offers of employment. Some people did see an opening earlier, take the risk, and receive the reward. Staying, too, had its reasons: familiar work, a steady income, apprehension about uncertainty, no clear judgment about the next step. I made that choice; I cannot cast myself as someone to whom a missed opportunity merely happened. Nor can one decision not to move account for everything that has disappointed me in my career. I have not always been sufficiently engaged. My enthusiasm for the work has faltered, my direction wavered. Some things within reach I did not pursue. At certain forks I stayed because moving seemed troublesome, because I feared the risk, or because I did not want it enough.

Yet “I did not try hard enough” would be another simplification. Who gets to own a project, which work happens to be recognized, how an organization perceives it, when an opening appears, what a choice comes to yield years later — much lies beyond individual intention. In retrospect, all these entangled circumstances shrink into a handful of sentences: I should have worked harder; I should have left; I should have taken the chance. As though, at certain moments, life had laid out the correct answers, and I needed only to choose them to arrive elsewhere.

It was probably never so clear. Knowing this does not diminish the attraction of the road not taken. Precisely because I never traveled it, its hazards, doubts, exhaustion, and accidents never had to happen. In memory it lies remarkably straight.

Investment invites comparisons of its own. Some people are genuinely better at it: their understanding of markets, assets, position sizes, and risk is sounder, and their returns are better. Others do little research and seldom attempt to predict the economy. They have simply kept to a basic passive allocation and, over the years, done well.

The first group makes me doubt my ability to judge: why can I not judge better? The second makes me doubt the urge itself: why must I judge so much? Both leave a third question: why did I not act in time? The reproaches are at odds. One demands more intelligence, another less cleverness, the third greater resolve. Yet they do not cancel one another. I can feel the force of all three.

Perhaps this is possible because I am not comparing myself with a whole person. The people I envy are different: a skillful investor, a patient follower of a simple plan, someone who joined a successful startup, someone who stayed at an established company and accumulated substantial long-term compensation. There may indeed be someone more successful in career, income, and investment, and more at ease than I am. His existence would be nothing extraordinary. But whether he exists is not quite the question.

### Imagination

I need not see anyone's account or establish what they have earned. It is enough to imagine someone beginning near where I began, seeing the opportunities earlier, making fewer mistakes at the decisive moments, and having more as a result. That place is already occupied in my mind. Classmates, colleagues, people I happen to hear about, the selves who did not sell, reverse course, or remain behind—any can stand there. Someone may actually approach this position, or occupy it fully. His existence is not what is at issue.

It is the place of an other for whom enjoyment seems complete: someone always knows more, chooses better, has more to enjoy. I install my ideal self there—cleverer, more decisive, wealthier, more at ease—and borrow a gaze from that position to turn upon my actual life. I aspire to become him while living under his scrutiny.

Fantasy here determines how opportunity, failure, and time appear to me. Once I believe that someone in comparable circumstances could have chosen better, uncertainty quietly changes its character. The market seems to have contained a correct answer all along; my career, a better route. He saw it and I did not. Chance becomes ability, a turn of fortune becomes an error of judgment, and regret becomes an indictment.

The roads not taken are especially susceptible to this change. They have no terrain, none of the insufficient knowledge, risk, fatigue, or hesitation of the time. Only the destination remains, known now. There is no journey along them, which is why they look straighter than the roads I have traveled. Judged from today's destination, yesterday's uncertain fork becomes a question I ought to have answered correctly.

Analysis, of course, cannot undo the facts. Some people invest better than I do. Some seized opportunities I missed. Had I adopted a simple, steady allocation earlier, I might well have more now. Psychoanalysis has a limited task here. It cannot explain away real differences as fantasy; it can only show how I pass from particular differences to that position from which someone always does better, and surrender the person I am to his judgment.

### Not Enough

Money is a ready medium for these comparisons because it gives them numbers. A vague unease acquires the appearance of precision: so much less earned in a given year, so much opportunity lost in a decision, so much more that another road might have brought.

The figures gradually compose an invisible ledger, accounting not merely for money but for the way these years have been spent. My salary is a score; a colleague's possibly higher salary is a score. Someone joins a startup and his equity appreciates: a score. Someone earns more by following a simple allocation: another. Money is made to answer questions beyond its competence: am I intelligent enough, have I succeeded, have I failed myself, have I missed the life I might have led?

A substantial sum added to my assets would not, in the near term, transform my daily life. The same sum lost would not immediately leave me in difficulty. Yet the feeling of insufficiency has not diminished with the growth of the account. My career may change, my family may face new responsibilities; these are real uncertainties for which capital provides a reserve. But it is also being asked to furnish proof. If money only had to sustain a life, some reasonably definite account of “enough” might be possible. Required also to prove that I am intelligent, have not fallen behind, have not missed too much, the number can scarcely come to rest.

The other person's position advances with my assets. Even at the figure I now imagine, he may remain ahead through an earlier career decision, a better investment, an easier life. The position holds precisely because it belongs to no particular person I can overtake. The more I chase it in numbers, the more measures I supply it with.

I want money largely for the freedom it affords. Yet if investment, comparison, and opportunity cost occupy my attention at work, over meals, and on my travels, money begins to consume the freedom it was meant to provide. This cannot be settled by an injunction to care less. Money matters; professional choices have consequences; investment belongs to adult life. Avoidance, waiting, and half-participation also exact a price. To pretend otherwise would be to fashion another image of myself above worldly concerns.

### The Road I Have Taken

The roads not taken will probably go on looking straight in retrospect. Some regrets are real; the sense of lack and guilt is real too. But those roads have no journey, while I am still in the midst of mine.

They have left their regrets. They need not go on making my decisions. What I truly want now, and what I am willing to undertake, remain questions to be answered here, on the road.
