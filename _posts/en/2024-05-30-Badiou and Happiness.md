> Happiness consists of enjoying the powerful and creative existence of something that seemed impossible. <br />
> — Alain Badiou

2023 was a year in which I read a great deal. Toward its end, as autumn in New England thinned into winter, I borrowed Alain Badiou’s *Happiness* from Hayden Library at MIT. It was my first sustained encounter with Badiou. Not long afterward I began Deleuze’s *Nietzsche and Philosophy*, and for a while it became natural to read the two against each other.

### Two Directions

Badiou and Deleuze are hardly two exhaustive alternatives for contemporary philosophy. Still, they pulled me in recognizably different directions.

From Deleuze came affirmation, becoming, multiplicity, the immanent power of life. From Badiou came rupture, decision, fidelity, the subject that emerges by committing itself to something that the situation could not already account for.

The contrast can easily be made too neat. Deleuze is not simply the philosopher of continuity, nor Badiou merely the philosopher of interruption. Yet as a way of reading - and perhaps more importantly, as a way of asking what I myself wanted from philosophy — the tension was useful.

I found Deleuze illuminating and, in a strange way, comforting. If difference and creation arise immanently from life itself, then perhaps life needs no external guarantee and no final tribunal. It moves, differentiates, becomes.

But there is a temptation hidden in this comfort. If becoming is already everywhere, if life is forever exceeding the forms imposed upon it, then where exactly does a decision begin? At what point must someone cease merely to describe the movement of forces and say: this is what I will stand by?

The same language of flow can sustain very different fantasies. One can dream of accelerating everything until the existing order breaks apart; one can also imagine that, since life is already becoming on its own, there is little need for me to do very much at all.

I do not think this is a refutation of Deleuze. It is simply where, for me, Badiou became necessary.

### Happiness

Badiou’s happiness is not contentment.

Contentment belongs easily enough to the world as it stands. One succeeds by its measures, acquires what it teaches one to desire, finds a tolerable place within it, and is satisfied. There is nothing contemptible about this. Much of ordinary life depends on such satisfactions.

But Badiou wants the word *happiness* for something more demanding.

A truth does not simply develop out of what is already known. Something happens that could not have been calculated from the existing arrangement — an encounter, an invention, a political rupture, a work, a love — and what matters thereafter is whether a subject can remain faithful to the possibilities opened by it.

This is why happiness, in Badiou, feels less like comfort than enlargement.

Something the world had presented as impossible becomes possible, not because the world has politely revised its rules, but because one has begun to live according to another measure. The finite life one already had is interrupted by a possibility that exceeds the place previously assigned to it.

I think this was what encouraged me when I first read him.

Not the promise that life could become painless. Not even the promise that a sufficiently heroic subject could overcome every limitation. What moved me was the refusal to identify happiness with successful adjustment.

There are moments when being better adapted to the world is not the answer, because the measure supplied by the world is itself what has become intolerable.

### The Risk

There is, admittedly, something severe in this account.

If happiness depends on fidelity to a possibility that cannot be guaranteed in advance, then there is no formula by which one can secure it. A rupture may fail. A conviction may prove mistaken. Fidelity may become stubbornness. What appears as courage from within one history may later look like folly.

Badiou does not remove this risk. In some sense, the risk is the point.

This also prevents me from simply converting his philosophy into another doctrine of self-improvement. “Be faithful to the event” could otherwise become one more command, one more image of the heroic person against which ordinary life is judged deficient. I am wary of that temptation.

Still, the distinction between happiness and satisfaction remains with me.

Most advice about happiness asks how one might live somewhat better within the life already given: how to want more wisely, suffer less unnecessarily, adjust expectations, cultivate gratitude. These are not trivial questions.

Badiou asks another one: what if, at certain moments, happiness begins precisely when the given measure of a life is no longer accepted as final?

This is also where his communism matters to me — not as a badge, still less as an assurance that history is moving toward the right conclusion, but as a refusal to let the existing distribution of possibilities declare itself natural and complete.

Perhaps that is why I found this little book so unexpectedly encouraging.

Real happiness, in this sense, is not the reward for having found one’s proper place in the world.

It is the experience of discovering that the place was never the limit of what a life could become.
