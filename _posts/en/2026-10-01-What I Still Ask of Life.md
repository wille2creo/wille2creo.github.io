---
title: What I Still Ask of Life
locale: en
excerpt: >-
  On reading Wittgenstein
---

I have found Wittgenstein difficult in a way that is not quite the difficulty of an obscure writer. Often I can follow his sentences, explain his examples, and even anticipate the misconception he is about to expose. Yet I remain unsure whether I have understood what matters. The explanation seems to leave me almost where I began.

A Chinese phrase kept occurring to me: to reach the utmost height and yet follow the ordinary way (极高明而道中庸). What is difficult, in reading him, is to perceive the height. His conclusions can sound so unremarkable that one wonders why it took philosophy to arrive at them. Knowing how to play chess does not require a rulebook to pass through the mind whenever one says “chess”. Looking for someone does not establish that the person exists. A picture does not carry its application within itself. These are things I can readily agree with. But agreement is cheap if I have not felt the attraction of the picture being questioned.

Consider the remark about grasping a rule. We feel that its future applications are already present in our understanding — not as effects causally determined by a present event, but “in some strange way”. Wittgenstein does not simply reject what we say. The expression he questions is precisely “in some strange way”. He recalls a child who wondered how a tailor could “sew clothes”, imagining that the whole garment was made by sewing alone, thread by thread. The mystery arose from a wrong picture attached to an ordinary expression. (Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics, I, §126.)

The precision lies in preserving what is intelligible while removing the imagined requirement. Wittgenstein does not dismiss the whole thought as nonsense, nor provide a hidden mechanism to satisfy it. He asks us to notice what we have made the words mean. Returning to ordinary usage is therefore not a retreat to something we already understood. What seemed familiar has to be examined again.

His remarks on natural laws require a related discrimination. He compares modern people stopping at natural laws with earlier people stopping at God and fate. The comparison need not erase the difference between scientific inquiry and an appeal to divine intention. Its force concerns the appearance that everything has now been explained. An explanation does not become a final account of the world merely because inquiry has stopped there. (Tractatus, §§6.371–6.372.)

There is, perhaps, an implicit ethics in this manner of philosophizing. It is not necessary to turn each logical difficulty into a personal predicament. The ethical seriousness lies in the attention demanded: the willingness to examine a thought even where its apparent depth is most compelling. Clarity may require giving up a satisfying picture without receiving another in exchange. This is a discipline of thought, not a general instruction to stop asking questions.

That seriousness takes a more explicitly ethical form in a notebook passage:

> Even the nearest future cannot be known with certainty. In short, there are moments when I can live not merely in reality, but for the spirit. We should regard the beautiful moments of life as grace, enjoy them with gratitude, and otherwise be indifferent to life.  
> — NB II, p. 24

Here I felt the depth more directly. I also saw that I did not inhabit the attitude the passage expressed.

The movement from uncertainty to gratitude is not a deduction. An uncertain future might occasion anxiety, resignation, or renewed effort; it does not logically compel the response Wittgenstein describes. What the passage articulates is a way of living with that uncertainty. The beautiful moments are to be enjoyed without their providing an assurance about the rest of life.

“Grace” matters. It gives the beautiful moment the character of something received rather than something guaranteed. Its goodness need not establish that everything is, or will become, good. Nor must it be diminished because it cannot last. Gratitude responds to the moment without requiring it to settle what lies beyond it.

This is more exacting than the familiar advice to cherish the present. That advice can suggest making the most of what is available. Here, enjoyment stands beside a much more austere injunction: “otherwise be indifferent to life.”

Those words should not be made comfortable too quickly. Taken as numbness, indifference would sit uneasily beside the enjoyment and gratitude just affirmed. Yet substituting a reassuring word such as “acceptance” would also soften the passage. How far does this indifference extend? How is it to coexist with care for particular people and things? The sentence does not answer these questions merely by being profound. They belong to the difficulty of understanding what it would mean to live in this way.

The passage also speaks of moments and of what we should do. It does not establish that its author possessed an uninterrupted serenity. There is no need to turn its clarity into a portrait of someone who had finally solved the problem of living. The thought may articulate an orientation that remains difficult even for the person expressing it.

This matters to the distance I felt while reading. Recognizing the force of an ethical remark is not the same as inhabiting its attitude. But neither does that distance mean the recognition was empty. A sentence can make a way of living intelligible before it becomes one’s own.

Here the phrase about height and the ordinary returns with a different emphasis. The ordinary need not be easy, and plain words need not express an easily attained composure. Wittgenstein’s remark offers neither an elaborate doctrine nor the reassurance that life will turn out well. Its difficulty survives our ability to explain it.

For now, I can say that these words struck me as profound, and that they made my distance from them visible. I would rather leave that distance visible than close it with a conclusion.
