The photographs from the past two weekends’ autumn outings near Boston are still on the camera’s memory card, not yet imported. Lightroom has 276 waiting for me, from late September’s visit to Acadia in Maine and the White Mountains in New Hampshire. And August’s trip to Alaska has become a 940-item task list on my hard drive, one I dare not open.

I seem to have become less diligent about editing since I came back from Alaska in late August:

I have books to read, games to play, and work to do;<br />
I get sleepy, stare into space, feel low;<br />
I want to create, want to escape, want to stop thinking.<br />
There really is no time to edit photos.

Not that this reluctance began in August. I did not get to the photos from the end of last year until mid-February. The camera’s images at least receive some care; my phone’s pictures have gone years without a proper sorting.

Then I cannot help reproaching my former self, the one who pressed the shutter: why did you take so many? Of course you enjoyed it; you weren’t the one who had to deal with them afterward. At times I have even considered hiring someone online to do the editing. But photography seems such a private affair — from the eye, to the forefinger on the shutter, to the act of looking at the screen afterward — that I cannot outsource it.

In fairness, I have not pressed the shutter all that often. The Sony I used for eight years, from 2016, has a shutter count of just 6,700. The Fujifilm I bought in June, even with a deliberate effort to shoot more, is only at 4,044. According to the maesters online, the way to improve is to keep shooting: ten thousand frames is barely a beginning, and thirty thousand a year nothing unusual.

But I have not yet learned how to press the shutter, and already I must begin learning how not to.
