These past few weeks I have been absorbed in games. I had company at the end of March; on the second weekend we took a day trip to Philadelphia, and I hardly played that week. But once I got back, I gave myself free rein and played through two nights.

So I have been short of sleep, increasingly restless and low in spirits. Yet even that “so” is open to question; what causes what is seldom clear when each feeds the other.

This week it has been *Death Stranding*. People sometimes say that playing games feels like working. In *Death Stranding*, it really is a job: I feel like an errand-runner, forever on my way to collect or deliver another parcel. That may be precisely what makes it so hard to stop. With a game like this, it is difficult to tell whether I keep playing because it is genuinely addictive, or because it lets me take refuge from one anxiety in another. Perhaps both. After all, we are always tempted to surrender our existence to something else.

Over the weekend I was still playing. For no apparent reason, a fragment began to recur in my mind: “But I did.” Perhaps it came from a puzzled remark made while consulting a walkthrough: “But I did build that bridge.” Repeating it seemed to summon a muted, bitter ache. The ordinary words took on disappointment, defensiveness, grievance, accusation. There was something of the familiar resentment of the weak in it.

“But I did.” It is as though someone else’s judgment is already in place before I begin. Whatever I have to say must start with “but”: a turn against something, not a beginning on its own terms. An “I” does enter through this objection, but the real work is done by the emphatic “did”. Rather than speaking on my own authority or setting the terms, I offer a further explanation, another assurance that what I say is true, in the hope of being acknowledged.

The strangeness of the phrase, the discomfort it brings me now, lies in the scene it seems to place me in. I arrive a moment late, adding a sentence after someone else has set the terms. I want to speak and seem compelled to speak this way, to win something back: I am not what you take me for; there is truth on my side, too, and you need to see it.

There is something suspiciously private in this reading, perhaps too much made of very little: once a person is anxious enough, even the most ordinary turn of speech can give off a cold gleam. The words themselves need not carry any of this. The world seems, rather, to have opened to me in the form of a misunderstanding. Correcting it in the language we all use merely shifts my position a little within the same prevailing view. So the ache and bitterness remain — nothing more than that fundamental condition called Unheimlichkeit. Let me be less hasty, then, to call this resentment; that judgment may itself conceal a cruel form of guilt.

The place from which the words are spoken is also where they are trying to arrive. They set out from somewhere within me, pass through who knows how much brooding, and come back to the same place. An implied listener accompanies them, with a different face in different circumstances — or sometimes the same face for years. This is puzzling: so many scenes and relationships, near and distant, before and after, so many ways of seeing myself are gathered here. Why should all this seem to have a single, definite face?

Perhaps it is simply laziness, though a laziness that deserves understanding and compassion. An entirety of relations is difficult to grasp; a face seems clear, something I may be able to address directly.

Perhaps the greater wisdom would be to leave this place as it is, empty and obscured. But I can think of a more modest expedient: if there must be a face there, let it be my own. That would already be difficult, and no small thing. Better still if, once there, I could restrain the urge to sit in judgment: simply hear the words I have spoken, and take responsibility for what I truly want.
