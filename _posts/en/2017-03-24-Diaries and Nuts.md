## The First Nut

When I was in high school, I had a vague picture of university life: an old campus, most of my time spent on a stone bench by the lake, a stack of paper and a few pens. There would be so much to write.

The picture was like a small nut, the first a young squirrel had ever found for itself. It might be too small to fill its belly, though perhaps the squirrel never meant to eat it. It kept the nut as a treasure, burying it secretly in the earth; forgetful as it was, it did not always remember what it had hidden. But when there seemed nowhere left to go, the thought that it still had this one small nut was like having the whole world at its back.

### Digression

When I left for university in 2014, I stubbornly refused to let my mother come with me. I thought this was independence. Remembering it now, I feel only an ache.

I would only go farther from home.

A few days ago I happened to rewatch several episodes of an old comedy set in an inn. Halfway through, I found myself wondering about the woman who ran it: she had come so far from home—why didn’t she go back? How did she manage without her mother and father there to love her? It was such a foolish thought, yet it would not leave me alone.

## Diaries

When I left for university in 2014, I took a notebook with me, intending to keep up my old habit of writing things down from time to time. The school had given it to me as a prize for doing well on an exam in my second year of high school. I had kept it tucked away, reluctant to use it; only just before leaving home did I write on its first page.

Nearly ten more notebooks lie scattered through the drawers back home. I gathered them together once, but somehow they have drifted apart again. For reasons I can no longer recover, following rules I can no longer recall, my younger self encoded certain things in obscure symbols on the page. Was he trying to hide something from the person I am now, or hoping I would remember? Whatever he intended, those blocks of symbols are now an unreadable script, scattered through drawers and lost beyond memory. Perhaps they will never be deciphered. They once held something. Now I can no longer see it.

But blanker still than these writings whose meaning is lost may be the hundreds of untouched pages I have left in the diary over the last three years. There is always something more important than keeping a diary, something more pleasant, something more meaningful. Somehow I always find a perfectly respectable reason to leave that warm yellow notebook standing on the shelf while the dust on it grows thick.

And yet I am never quite reconciled to it. Not to those great stretches of blank paper, not to joy and sorrow left indistinct, least of all to a life reduced to mere survival. A diary may not be able to bear all the hopes I place in it, but it is still my innermost refuge. One day I wrote:

> You ask why I have taken to keeping a diary lately. To leave a record, something my future self might smile over. Never mind the faint redness around my eyes today, nor on the clouds that have drifted away. Only to write a little more, as best I can. Will you understand?
