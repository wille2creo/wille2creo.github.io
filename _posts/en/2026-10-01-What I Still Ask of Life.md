title: What I Still Ask of Life

  On reading Wittgenstein

> In fact it is already a seed of good originality not to want to be what you are not.  
> —Wittgenstein, *Culture and Value*

I have found Wittgenstein difficult in a way that is not quite obscurity. Often a sentence is plain enough; what is difficult is to see why so much turns on it. Knowing how to play chess does not require its rules to pass through the mind whenever one says “chess.” A picture does not, by itself, tell us how to apply it. Agreement comes readily. Recognising the hold of the picture being questioned takes longer.

A phrase kept coming to mind: *to reach the utmost height, yet follow the ordinary way*. The difficulty was to perceive the height. Wittgenstein often seems to return us to something we already knew, yet the return is not innocent. What looked like a deeper requirement may turn out to be something we had supplied ourselves.

His remarks on natural laws turn on such a distinction. He compares modern people stopping at natural laws with earlier people stopping at God and fate. The comparison does not make scientific inquiry equivalent to an appeal to divine intention. It questions the appearance that everything has now been explained. Even answers to every scientific question, he writes, would leave the problems of life untouched. (*Tractatus* 6.371–6.372, 6.52)

The ethical significance of this boundary is not that another, higher science must take over. In the *Tractatus*, value cannot be one more fact among the things that happen. Nor can philosophy claim exemption from the limits it draws: the book’s own propositions are to be overcome, its ladder discarded. The concluding silence is severe, but it does not dismiss what cannot be formulated as worthless. In the *Lecture on Ethics*, Wittgenstein treats the impulse to “run against the boundaries of language” with profound respect, even while denying that it can yield a science of absolute value.

The later philosophy changes the question. Rather than asking every expression to conform to a single model of representation, it attends to the activities in which words have their lives. Requesting, thanking, greeting and praying appear among the language-games of the *Philosophical Investigations*. Not every utterance is an attempt to describe something. (§23)

This matters to ethical speech. An apology need not merely report an inward feeling. Someone is addressed; something is acknowledged or evaded; the words may be followed by an attempt at repair. Their seriousness cannot be detached from those circumstances. Looking for it solely behind the words would miss what is happening between the people who speak them.

Neither does ordinary practice settle what is right. Evasion and cruelty have familiar ways of speaking too. Returning to the ordinary calls for closer discrimination, not deference to whatever happens to be customary. What I find implicitly ethical in this attention is its refusal to let an impressive formulation do the work of looking. The comparison of philosophical methods to therapies suggests the same patience with particular entanglements. It offers no general cure for living, and suffering is not necessarily a conceptual mistake. (*Philosophical Investigations* §133)

The question becomes what such clarity asks of the person seeking it. A remark in *Culture and Value* brings this into focus without turning it into a doctrine. Wittgenstein distinguishes even the finest taste from creative power. Sensitivity receives and discriminates; it does not thereby make anything. He then admits uncertainty about himself: he knows that he has taste, but cannot clearly tell whether he has originality. Perhaps one sees what one possesses, he suggests, rather than what one is.

This passage touched me. Its conclusion is almost disconcertingly modest: do not want to be what you are not. One expects originality to require an addition—a distinctive voice, an invention, something unmistakably one’s own. Wittgenstein begins with the falsification that can be refused.

Honesty cannot substitute for making the work, any more than appreciation can. But neither is originality secured by assuming an original-looking manner. The passage leaves creative ability uncertain while making the next question less obscure: not how to certify one’s originality, but how to begin without pretence. Even its final admission, that others have said all this better, belongs to the thought. A remark about originality declines to make novelty its claim upon us.

That restraint matters when the words we admire describe a way of living. Another passage, from the wartime notebooks, moved me more directly:

> One should enjoy the good hours of life gratefully, as a blessing, and otherwise feel indifferent toward life.  
> —Wittgenstein, *Private Notebooks*

I also saw quite plainly that I did not inhabit the attitude it expressed.

The surrounding reflection begins with the uncertainty even of the nearest future. But the movement from uncertainty to gratitude is not a deduction. Uncertainty might occasion anxiety, resignation or renewed effort. Wittgenstein articulates an orientation toward life rather than a conclusion compelled by evidence.

To receive the good hours as a blessing is to enjoy them without requiring them to establish anything about the hours that follow. Their goodness need not guarantee that everything will turn out well. Nor must it be diminished because it cannot last. This is more exacting than advice to make the most of the present: enjoyment is preserved, while its claim upon the rest of life is relinquished.

The injunction to indifference remains harder. Read as numbness, it sits uneasily beside gratitude and enjoyment. Yet replacing it with a comfortable word such as *acceptance* would soften its severity. How is such indifference to coexist with care for particular people and things? The sentence does not answer that question merely by being profound.

Nor does its clarity establish that its author always lived within it. It speaks of how one should live, not of an uninterrupted serenity already attained. To turn it into the portrait of someone beyond struggle would add something the words themselves do not warrant.

Here the remark on originality returns. Admiring an attitude is not the same as possessing it. To adopt its voice as though I had already attained its composure would be a peculiar response to the injunction not to be what I am not. Yet the distance does not make the recognition empty. A way of living can become intelligible before it becomes one’s own.

This is where I begin to discern the height in the ordinary. The difficulty is not exhausted when the sentence has been explained. Sometimes what remains concerns the use of a word; sometimes it concerns whether words we understand have a place in the way we live. These are not the same problem, but reading Wittgenstein has brought them close enough for the distinction itself to matter.

An intellectual life might illuminate itself without illuminating everything. I think of morning light, partly obscured by cloud: neither a spotlight in which to appear nor a searchlight that leaves nothing unexamined. Things become visible without everything becoming transparent. Such a light would not abolish the work of speaking, judging and acting, or do that work in our place.

For now, these remarks make visible both an orientation and my distance from it. I can acknowledge their force without claiming their composure. That seems a more truthful beginning than finding words to close the distance.
