### Breaking Even

This spring, an investment I had held for more than half a year finally recovered to around my purchase price. I sold the entire position at once.

It had been falling for a long time. I had my reasons for buying; once the price fell, however, I neither added to the position nor left it. I simply held on. Over those months my attention drifted from the investment itself to the figures in my account: how far below cost, how long until it recovered, whether I might at least leave without a loss. The moment the price crossed that line and a small profit reappeared, I sold, as though permission to leave had finally been granted. Before long, it rose a great deal further.

There is nothing unusual in this. The market is full of people who sell too soon and watch prices climb without them. What troubles me is the disproportionate reality my purchase price acquired. The market knows nothing of what I paid; that line exists only between my account and my memory, and the price will not pause there on my behalf. Yet for as long as I held the investment below cost, something felt unfinished. Only when it recovered did I feel at liberty to leave.

“Breaking even” appears to concern money alone. Hidden in it is a demand on time: erase the minus sign, restore things to the moment before the error, let the decision have left no consequences. Prices can return to where they were. Time cannot.

Years ago, in a highly volatile market, I nearly lost my entire account trying to recover a short-term loss I could have afforded to accept. The initial loss was small, but I would not let the account finish in the red. I traded repeatedly, with leverage far beyond what my experience or tolerance could justify. At one point I came close to breaking even; stopping there would have ended the matter. I insisted instead on erasing the last of the loss. A few days later, one sharp, momentary price swing took almost all my capital.

The experience left me wary of leverage, certainly, and afraid of it. But I also noticed how the first small loss had refused to become the past. Whether the next trade made sense was no longer the foremost question: it had been charged with repairing the one before it. Unable to accept a negative ending, I kept mortgaging the future to the past.

The pattern has returned in other forms. Once an investment is losing money, I want to wait until it is at least near cost before leaving; once it recovers, I am liable to leave immediately. A historical figure becomes a threshold. Below it, I remain the person who chose wrongly. Beyond it, I am briefly innocent again.

There is ordinary loss aversion here, but I seem always to want back a little more than money. I want the self who had not yet made the mistake, who had not been marked by the minus sign.

I have not brought every position back to even, nor could I. Some high-risk trades ended in substantial losses, leaving me chiefly to regret not cutting them sooner. Other investments recovered after I had cut my losses, returning to cost and sometimes rising further; then regret reversed itself: why had I not held on? The investment I began with is a third case—recovery, immediate departure, and a further rise watched from outside.

These experiences cannot together yield a rule that hindsight will always vindicate. Cutting a loss offers no guarantee of a better outcome; one may sell at a temporary low. It lends itself to a rule because a boundary to the risk can at least be set in advance. That prices will sometimes recover after one has left is part of its cost. Breaking even is hardly a plan in the same sense. My purchase price belongs to my history; the market owes it no return. “I will decide when I break even” is often less a decision than a wish.

### Believing Early

This rally is not necessarily over, but I have probably missed much of its rapid rise and repricing. Had I arrived earlier at a reasonably settled judgment and begun to invest, I would at least have some gains beneath me now. Further fluctuations would present a question about what I already held. Remaining outside, I have to ask at each new high whether it is already too expensive.

These are different states of mind. Yesterday it looked expensive, so I did not buy; today it rises and looks more expensive still. Months pass, high follows high, and my original caution leaves me finding it ever harder to act. When at last I resolve to buy, the market may really begin to fall, seeming to vindicate the fear that kept me out.

Over the past two years I have indeed been too cautious, too slow, too burdened by the need for reasons. An action seems to require a case complete enough to support it. The market does not wait for that case to be complete.

Yet “believe early” readily becomes another clever remark made after the fact. I cannot rewrite myself as someone who saw the prospect clearly and merely lacked the courage to bet on it. I did not, in fact, believe that strongly then.

For all my scientific and technical training, I have little enthusiasm for technology as such, and less faith in progress. My reservations about technological expansion may put me closer to Laozi or Heidegger. At the same time, I am drawn to an accelerationist possibility: that AI might carry the existing movement of capital and technology into a more thoroughgoing imbalance.

An interest in historical rupture is not a settled investment conviction. And in the daily business of watching prices, my reasons are usually much simpler. It has risen so far already—is it too late? Would waiting bring a better entry? When a correction comes, I wonder whether a larger decline has only just begun. Laozi, Heidegger, and accelerationism recede. There remains someone watching a changing price, reluctant to accept that it might fall the moment he buys.

So I did not believe then, and I did not act.

What is worth retaining in “believe early” may amount only to this: a judgment cannot wait for uncertainty to vanish before becoming an action. Early conviction may be mistaken; an early position may lose money. The point is simply one I have found difficult to accept: when the time comes to act, the evidence is still incomplete.

### Back and Forth

A consequential trading mistake last year took this in another direction. Markets had fallen sharply following a policy change. I had begun adding to my long-term holdings and made plans to continue gradually. After reading a great deal of bearish analysis, I abruptly reversed my view: not only abandoning the original position, but taking a leveraged position in the opposite direction. The market recovered. Committed now to my choice, I kept finding reasons to hold it and took a long time to leave.

There was a real loss. Heavier still was the imagined opportunity cost: had I stayed with the original plan, how much more would I have now? In the ledger of regret, money that never entered my account was recorded alongside money actually lost.

For a time I called the mistake arrogance. Pessimism offers an enticing vantage point: others see only the rise; I see the danger they have missed. They follow the market's mood; I preserve the lucidity of the few. Since risk is never absent, a capable analyst can always find grounds for caution. To see a danger others have overlooked affords its own quiet superiority.

Yet to say now that I have finally seen through my arrogance is another small elevation of myself. My former self thought he saw more clearly than the market; the present one imagines he sees through his predecessor. An image of greater maturity and insight soon occupies the same place. There were genuine risks then, and my doubts were not without foundation. Had the market moved the other way, I might now be giving a different account of myself. The outcome has no authority to return to the past and declare all my misgivings foolish.

A short-term view, held without much conviction, had acquired the power to overturn a long-term plan; the position I took exceeded the confidence my judgment deserved. To explain everything by arrogance as a defect of character may obscure this more particular failure of practice.

The circumstances of the other trades vary, but their rhythm is much the same. A falling price shakes my confidence; a loss makes me reluctant to leave; recovery sends me hurrying for the exit; a further rise leaves me regretting the departure. I once called all of this risk aversion. What I seem less able to bear, however, is ordinary uncertainty without a prompt conclusion: a little loss after buying, a trade that ends in the red, a market whose position I still cannot determine. To close these open cases, I sometimes resort to more extreme action.

Recently the cycle has compressed itself into minutes. I buy, then worry that I might have paid less or that the fall will continue, and promptly sell. Having sold, I wonder whether I have chosen a relative low, and buy again. The first price becomes an anchor: buying back ought to cost a little less than the sale brought in; selling again ought to bring a little more than the purchase cost. Otherwise a few minutes of hesitation appear to crystallize into a loss.

This is price anchoring, but not simply the wish for a better return. To buy back above my sale price feels like admitting that selling was a mistake; to sell below my entry feels like admitting that buying was one. The second move ought to undo the first, preferably on slightly better terms. But the market does not reserve a place where a decision can be withdrawn without cost.

Hesitation has its price, as resolve has its price. Buying entails a possible further fall; selling, a possible immediate rebound. A genuine change of mind may require buying back at a higher price. A decision becomes a decision precisely by closing certain possibilities.

What this first exposes is a lack of method and conviction. Why enter, what facts would warrant a change of mind, how much fluctuation to accept: none is sufficiently clear. Price itself becomes the strongest evidence, every movement enough to reopen the case. Yet an account of my investment process does not quite exhaust the difficulty. I cannot readily let a decision stand while it remains unproven. Before acting, I want more evidence; afterward, I want the market's prompt assent. Failing that, I want to withdraw, and preferably owe nothing for the withdrawal.

Seen this way, waiting months to break even and changing course within ten minutes are not so distant. One waits for the market to restore the starting point; the other begins looking for a way back almost as soon as the move is made. After a setback, the wish to gain can recede before the more urgent wish to restore.

The line marking my cost gives this wish an especially persuasive form. The price really can return; it begins to seem that time might follow. Yet even when the account comes back to even, the decision has not ceased to have happened.

### In Practice

What can actually change remains modest. Before buying, I need to accept that my judgment will not be fully established; afterward, to allow it some time amid ordinary fluctuations. When the facts change, I need to bear the cost of revising it. That may mean realizing a loss, paying more to return, or watching prices recover after I have left. The next action need not recover the losses of the last, nor demonstrate that its predecessor could have been withdrawn for nothing.

None of this will spare me further losses, or necessarily improve my returns. To treat it as a way of finally avoiding error would merely relocate the ideal self: no longer the person who always calls the market correctly, but the perfectly disciplined investor who never loses control. The aversion to error would persist, dressed in greater maturity.

When another investment returns to cost after a long loss, I will probably still hear the familiar “at last,” and may still want to sell. I will not pretend that my entry price means nothing to me. I only want to close the page, leave it for a day, and then ask: if I did not own this position, would I take it now, at today's price, with what I now know? And if my judgment has changed, am I willing to pay for the change?

The cost line will remain in my account. But perhaps it will be only an old price, no longer a point to which time permits me to return.
