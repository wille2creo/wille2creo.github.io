### Contemplation

In *The Secret of the Golden Flower*, Jung describes a curious difficulty. Some people, he says, have made progress and found a measure of freedom by doing nothing: they simply let things happen. Borrowing from Meister Eckhart, he speaks of releasing our hold on ourselves, and arrives at the need to “let things happen in the psyche.” Yet this is precisely where the difficulty begins. The conscious mind must always intervene: help, correct, reject. First it dismisses the images that arise of their own accord as foolish, dull, or fruitless; once past that objection, it cannot resist interpreting them, classifying them, embellishing or belittling them. Jung calls this a “cramp of consciousness,” and speaks of curbing the mind's impatience.

Thinking is a habit of mine. A thought arises, and merely to notice its arrival seems insufficient: having come this far, I ought to follow it a little further; having found a cause, I ought to seek the cause behind it. Pain, especially, takes on the character of unfinished work. Another pass might yield a different explanation, perhaps even a resolution. Yet much of what I have thought about has grown no clearer for all that thought. It has only been thought about once more.

This brings to mind Buddhist contemplation, though the two are not the same. The Chinese text of *The Secret of the Golden Flower* belongs to a Daoist tradition; Jung, in turn, reads it through a different tradition. At least the contemplation I have in mind is not the kind of looking in the Greek and European tradition that sets an object before us for close inspection, nor the comprehensive view sought by Goethe or Wittgenstein. In Buddhist terms, one could speak of attending to how things arise through conditions and seeing that they have no fixed nature, clinging to nothing; then, from that emptiness, turning again to the world in all its variety. Such an account can be fluent, even profound.

When a thought arises, how, exactly, am I to observe it? To contemplate “myself” seems to require a lucid, stable self to keep watch, with thoughts arriving as disturbances to that position. To contemplate the thoughts, on the other hand, is all too easily to follow them wherever they lead. The difficulty is in stopping.

The Chinese text is quite practical here. Looking at the tip of the nose means to “take a single look when first entering stillness, establish a guide, then let it go”—like a mason hanging a line: “he does not keep staring at the line.” When a thought arises, one should “examine where it is, where it comes from, and where it passes away.” Yet after repeated examination has found nothing to grasp, the instruction is: “do not go looking for its source again.” Finally: “Let stillness be followed by observation, and observation by stillness.”

I do not yet understand much of the practice described here. But “do not go looking for its source again” gives me pause. It is not a prohibition on thought: the preceding instruction plainly calls for repeated examination. There does, however, seem to be a point beyond which inquiry ceases to be observation and becomes the mind's own activity once more. There is always another layer, and another beneath it, as though sufficient thought would bring us at last to an explanation that left nothing over. Perhaps this is where the mind's impatience lies.

### The Thunderstorm

Jung has another image I am fond of. Certain problems are not resolved on their own logical terms; rather, as life acquires a new and stronger direction, they gradually lose their hold. Seen from a higher stage of personal development, the old, fierce conflicts resemble a thunderstorm in a valley, viewed from a mountaintop. But he immediately adds that, inwardly, we are the valley as well as the mountain. The storm has not vanished. We are still shaken and tormented by it, even as we can say: “I know that I suffer.”

“To be in a fog without knowing it, and to know that one is in a fog—these are more than a thousand miles apart.”

I am drawn to this state. Even to call it something I long for is risky, though: the mind can make it another goal, as if enough practice would secure me a permanent home on the summit, with no more descents into the rain. Jung seems already to have ruled this out. We are both the mountain and the valley. I do, of course, wish not to suffer; I also know how little such a wish can promise. Perhaps there could at least be less suffering over suffering itself. Or, put another way: I can suffer, and still be happy.

Which brings me back to Jung: “let things happen in the psyche.”

### The Doctor

I think, too, of the Tenth Doctor in *Doctor Who*, who seems particularly unable to leave a thought alone. Not simply because there is always someone to save, but because this is how he lives: the slightest anomaly, an unfinished sentence, an unexplained detail, and his mind is off. Yet he is also perpetually traveling, from one planet to another, from one adventure into the next. I sometimes wonder whether these are not, in fact, the same thing. With something new always waiting to be thought about, he need not pause over what no amount of thinking can resolve. The Time War, loss, loneliness, farewells: none of these will yield to a little more thinking. And the universe is vast. The TARDIS can always take off again.
