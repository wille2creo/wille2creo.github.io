Lately I have been taking a bath now and then in the evening. The bathroom light is a little harsh, but switching it off leaves me unable to see anything at all.

Eventually I found a lamp that had been sitting unused. With that on and the shower curtain partly drawn, it was much more comfortable.

Tonight, drying myself after another bath, I became aware of the quiet around me and the softness of the light. Without quite knowing why, I thought of the summers I spent as a child at the factory in Guangdong where my parents worked.

Not a particular scene or even a clear image: more as though a mist of memory had settled into the light, bringing with it the familiar, faintly acrid smell of an old air conditioner. A summer holiday was long in those days. The wall-mounted unit would often begin to flag before half the summer was out, and a man would arrive on a motorcycle, carrying refrigerant to service it.

Afterward the room was cool again, and I jumped on the bed with all the more energy, only to work up another sweat. Perhaps the bed back home had hard wooden boards, or perhaps it was simply less satisfying to jump on. In any case, jumping on the bed was part of the novelty of Guangdong.

Sweating meant another wash. I remember a long, poorly lit path between the bedroom and the bathroom, open to the sky and frightening to a child. I would run there, wash in a hurry, then run back faster still, dust clinging to my feet before I had dried them.

Not every evening was spent jumping on the bed. Sometimes I watched television with my parents and listened to them talk; sometimes my mother and I danced, laughing and making up the steps until we were both exhausted. On evenings when they were busy—perhaps with chemical experiments, perhaps making products—and came back late, I read by myself, mostly storybooks. I read *The Little Mermaid* several times, fascinated and close to tears. I have not read it since. Waves, sunlight, colored foam: these are what I dimly remember.

The nights were not always peaceful either. The blanket was small, and the bed creaked whenever I turned over too vigorously. As I drifted off, a family of mice would begin moving about above the ceiling. I had no aching back then, and woke each morning with energy to spare. My parents, I see now, must have been exhausted. My mother has spoken of the earlier years at an electronics factory: a twelve-hour shift, then out to run a stall at the night market. Such a hard life. How could it have been so hard?

My hair has long since dried as I write this. I should stop here. After a day's work, I find myself missing home. When I will make one, or where, I still do not know. I always feel like a stranger, never quite settled, and so have little wish to furnish the place where I happen to live.
