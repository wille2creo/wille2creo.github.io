### Beginning

It has been a while since I wrote anything. Not for want of something to say: there are thoughts enough, but when I come to the page I do not know where to begin. Somewhere in this there remains a quiet expectation—perhaps writing it down will make it clear.

When I was younger, a couple of essays and a few concepts put in order could seem to settle something. There would be consolation, encouragement, or a place in thought for a feeling that had previously been obscure. These were not without use. Later, though, the things I thought about drew closer to my life and would no longer remain quietly on the page. Certain dangers, anxieties, and predicaments ceased to be objects I could set before me and discuss. I lived within them, subject to their tangled pull, and came to hope that thought and expression might actually resolve something. Writing offers no such assurance.

I do not think it makes things more confused, either. The confusion is there already. Unwritten, it can remain a dim awareness, something I need not yet touch. Pen in hand, unable to get a word down, I discover how substantial it is. There was no essay concealed within me, waiting to be transferred to the page.

There is a real labor of making here. To bring something out of nothing—not just anything, but something faithful to one's actual circumstances—is hardly a simple undertaking. I have at times forced myself to begin, passed through the initial difficulty, and arrived at a few sentences I could recognize as my own. What had been obscure acquired a discernible shape. Expression, release, even therapy: none is an altogether empty name.

But clarity exacts a price. I think of the opening of the *Laozi*: without desire, we see its mystery; with desire, we see its bounds. Before being written, things retain a certain wholeness, not necessarily compatible but not yet excluding one another. On the page they take a direction, like an arrow loosed: to fly this way is no longer to fly elsewhere. Something is expressed, while something else is worn away, shaped, distorted, perhaps lost. What exists all at once in its profusion has to pass through words and sentences into a line that can be read in sequence.

This is not an argument for silence. The unspent possibilities of what has not been said are no better than the sentences that have. Reluctance to forgo another direction does not entitle us to count every unloosed arrow a perfect shot.

Nor, to digress a little, do I think it much of an objection merely to point out that an account leaves something out. Someone makes a point; someone else reminds them of the other side, as though the truth of that side relieved us of having to speak from this one. I know there is another side. But once this side, that side, all the sides conceived and still unconceived have been accommodated, one's own meaning may have been accommodated out of existence.

Some relinquishment is a condition of expression. Where the writing bears directly on one's life, this easily passed-over condition begins to weigh. I do wish to finish an essay. I do not wish to take the ease of its sentences as evidence that the matters within them have been settled.

### Therapy

This year I have left most of my coding to AI and begun to ask its help with writing. Unable to begin this essay, I tried speaking first: I talked, it listened, occasionally offering a prompt. At least something of what was in my mind could be brought into words.

A curious difficulty arises here. I acknowledge that some of my writing is therapeutic. But how is therapeutic writing to be done with AI?

“Therapy” is a word I have never much liked, though I do not mean to disown it. Wittgenstein compared philosophical methods to therapies, and the philosopher's work on a problem to treating an illness. This was not the construction of another grand theory, but attention to the habits of thought and language in which we become entangled. Writing is not that philosophy. In a related, limited sense, however, it can be therapeutic: what is knotted in the mind has to be spoken before I begin to discern what I think, what I intend, perhaps even how I shall act. Pain may remain. But to be lost in a fog without knowing it, and to know that one is in a fog, are different conditions.

Still, how does one do this with AI? The objection is not that AI cannot suffer—it may, though the origins and manner of its suffering need not resemble yours. It may even address a pain with great precision, but not the pain for which you sought help. The person its sentences console may be someone else, composed within the writing; the difficulty they resolve may not be the one you inhabit. You may never have suffered in that way, yet receive, in an essay that seems remarkably apt, the remedy for that suffering.

This is not to divide the matter neatly between my pure, private feelings and a machine arranging words. My own feelings have also come from elsewhere. Success and failure, envy and shame: there were names for these before me. I grew up among them, learning to recognize and fashion myself through them, learning what to take pleasure in and what to suffer over. The most private part of a life is not outside language or society. A sigh does not become untaught simply because it leaves my mouth.

And yet the body is still here. However coded or constructed, the fatigue, unease, and restlessness remain sensations in my body. Pain in a text may receive an apt name, a paragraph arrive at a settled full stop; I remain where I am, in the body. Another person's words have moved me before. Borrowed language is not on that account false. But reading an expression already accomplished is not the same as struggling to accomplish it oneself: the hesitation, the admission resisted, the words scarcely spoken before they have to be taken back and tried again.

Several times I interrupted the AI. It told me that the exploration might itself be enough, that the thoughts were profound, that there was no need to hurry toward a final answer. I was not disputing what should count as enough. I was up against a particular difficulty, and I wanted to write this essay. If I speak of pain, I do not need a demonstration that speaking of pain is also a good thing, or consolation concerning the value of thought. I am concerned with what, precisely, I am saying—and what I am not.

My relation to technology, as I have said elsewhere, is not of one piece. I can be stirred by radical accelerationist possibilities, by the prospect of a break in the existing distribution of wealth and the social order. Whether it would lead to an advance or to destruction I do not know; the possibility still excites me. Yet by temperament I am close to Laozi and Heidegger, with a homesickness I cannot quite name. Technology grows more efficient. Life need not become better for it, nor need we become any clearer about how to live.

To return to therapy: the word troubles me also because it presupposes a wound, a lack to be made good, something awaiting repair. Perhaps my resistance comes from having already accepted this picture too fully—as though I must first establish what is broken in me before I have grounds to act; as though writing must always circle an absence, everything it produces offered in compensation.

I would rather create. Creation need not begin by proving that I have been wounded, or find a deficiency before granting me leave to write. I want to bring something new into being, not merely restore in words what has been lost.

### Escape and Departure

There is a related distinction between escape and departure.

Escape remains directed toward a place one cannot bear to stay: this is wrong, this hurts, therefore I must leave. To set out need not begin in refusal. Whether things are good or bad here is not the first concern; there are other places, things that have yet to happen. That may be reason enough to go.

It has been a long time since I clearly tasted the latter. Work, investment, ordinary life, even travel have often been attempts to extricate myself: leave this predicament, evade those demands, gain a little respite from an entanglement. What I am going toward is less distinct. Sometimes a journey is fully arranged and I still feel only that I am leaving, not that I have set out.

Escape is not shameful. There are places where one ought not to remain. But when so much of life proceeds from “not this, not anymore,” the question of what I do want grows lonely.

At eighteen or nineteen, perhaps earlier, I had considerable faith in the person I would become. Give him a few years, I thought, and surely he would understand, surely he would know how to manage things. His inward life, above all, would have advanced. Perhaps he would find peace; perhaps he would be vigorous amid the turmoil. I preferred the latter: water in a storm, surging without ceasing to be water.

Approaching thirty, I have found neither. Neither peace nor that rising force, but wear, fatigue, a restlessness that finds no place to settle. Restlessness does not confer the power to act. Sometimes it leaves one unable either to stay or to depart.

I had thought that these years would at least make what I wanted clearer. My younger self entrusted so much to the one who would follow him. I know now that the later self need not have any better idea what to do.

### Four Years and Thirteen

Eventually, in speaking, I came to the trauma of 2022. Four full years have passed, a little more. Why does it still hold me?

It is not only the continuing pain I find hard to accept, but its proportion to the time available. A life has few years. Since childhood I have entertained the unfounded notion that I would not live very long—to fifty, perhaps. There is no reason to settle on that number. Yet on that reckoning, as I approach thirty, only twenty-odd years remain. If twenty-five is the midpoint, these four years have already consumed nearly a sixth of the second half.

The endpoint is imagined. The four years are real.

Here I stopped and cried for a while.

To another person, what happened might hardly count as trauma. Often I myself think it carries no great weight. I find no profound meaning in it, no profound bond that would warrant all this looking back and interpreting. But it has lingered for four years; must it linger still? I will not lend it significance merely because it refuses to leave, so as to make the expenditure of those years appear worthwhile. To have occupied years is not to have deserved them.

Something else happened in 2013. It too once seemed indelible. Thirteen years on, only an image or two remains, with little particular feeling attached. Here, apparently, is consolation ready to hand: time does let things pass; four years and another nine will also make thirteen.

But the feelings did not take thirteen years to fade. A year or two, three or four at most, and they had gone. Thirteen years is only the distance from which I look back. Far from consoling me, the comparison sharpens the question: why does one thing once so close withdraw of its own accord, while after four years with this one I seem still to be standing here?

I was different thirteen years ago. There was life and energy in me; I could be hurt and recover. At least in memory, I did not go on making the experience into a trauma. Life opened elsewhere instead of turning endlessly around it. Looking back, I might say that I knew how to let myself be.

What sort of act is that, though—letting oneself be?

Let go, people say. Leave yourself in peace. The words are plain; attempting to follow them, I cannot find the thing to put down or the place where my hand should loosen. As though willingness were enough for something long gripped to fall away. I do not even know how I am holding it, or what besides my hand maintains the grip.

It is difficult. I am trying. Yet this seems not to be something effort can accomplish. There may be no way to try, or trying may only tighten the knot.

### Fallen Leaves

Thinking of high school brought back something else, apparently unconnected.

Shortly after we began our first year, the school organized a cleanup. A classmate and I were sweeping leaves, each with a large bamboo broom. It was an old campus with great camphor trees. I suddenly asked her why the fallen leaves had to be swept away.

I have forgotten the actual words. Something to this effect: autumn comes and leaves lie on the ground; underfoot they rustle, and rain falling on them makes another sound. The yellow slowly changes, darkens, wears away, returns at last to the soil. Would it not be good to let them stay?

I said it and forgot. A passing remark—who would keep it?

In our final year, I think, with graduation and parting near, she brought it up. She had always remembered what I said about the leaves, had thought it unusual and interesting.

Fifteen years later I remember neither her name nor her face.

I remember that she remembered my words. I cannot remember who she was.

I think I first heard Pu Shu's “Those Flowers” in middle school. On arriving at university, we were introducing ourselves, and somehow the icebreaker involved singing. I sang that song then too: “Where are they now? I was lucky to have grown alongside them.”

I have always kept apart and had few friends. I was more outgoing in the early years, but just as solitary, and largely by choice. I do not consider this a bad thing, nor wish to furnish the past with a sociability it never had. There were few friends, but in those shared years there were people whose lives crossed mine.

Later we went to different countries and cities, acquired different circumstances, ceased to accompany one another through our present lives. All quite natural. Parting troubles me, but I am not incapable of accepting it. To lose even the names and faces, though, is to lose something further.

The AI suggested that it was as though nothing of that time remained.

Of course not, I said immediately. Look: I still remember this.

The leaves remain, the remark remains, her remembering it remains. It is not that everything has vanished; what remains is precisely what makes me weep. Were nothing left, I would have no way of knowing what I had lost. Nor is it simply grief. These things can still take me back somewhere, and there I discover how much I have changed.

Suppose I met her again today: recognition, greetings, the customary exchange. What would I most wish to say?

Not, I think, to speak again of leaves. I would say how different I have become from the person she knew and remembered. Those young, living responses, that spontaneous interest in what lay before me, have grown rare. My concerns have changed. Much that I once felt without effort is now known to me only through memory: so that was how I could once feel.

I regret this.

### Guilt

Not because growing up ought to exempt me from worldly concerns. To live here is to face responsibilities, earn money, work. There is nothing wrong with that. Missing the fallen leaves is no reason to pose, belatedly, as someone who has always lived above such things. Life has its claims. I know them.

But why to this extent?

Why should anxiety over the future, success, and money leave so little room for anything else that I cannot be sure my younger self would recognize me? I do not wish to preserve the adolescent intact. But some things that might have continued to grow seem to have been denied the room.

I call this a betrayal, a debt incurred in the very manner of living. It points to no particular wrong I can enumerate; it concerns how I have spent these years, what I have become, what once had life in it and gradually lost its place.

Change I understand. Acceptance of change, as such, does not seem difficult. But we tend to expect, however vaguely, an improvement: that innocence relinquished will give way to another richness, that lost sensitivity will bring some degree of calm. No rule guarantees this exchange. Sometimes loss is only loss, with nothing of equal worth arriving in its place. To know there is no such guarantee, and to live that knowledge as helplessness, seem here to be the same thing.

The AI offered consolation: perhaps the guilt itself showed that this part of me had not wholly disappeared.

I admit that it consoled me. But I do not wish that part of myself to survive only as a debt, or what was once alive to retain only the power to reproach me. These things were never meant to sit in judgment on me. They were an interest I could feel, a remark made without preparation, something already absorbing before I thought to ask its meaning.

I would like them still to do some good. Not return each time merely to tell me how much has been lost.

### Home

I seem to cry more often lately. This need not be a bad thing. I know from experience that tears, when they come, can be better than their refusal.

I know their refusal too: the confusion within, or the numbness, the wish that the glands would at least produce a little water and loosen something. Nothing comes.

Later we spoke of home.

I have not called my family often in recent years—rarely, one might say. Does that mean I do not miss home? But what home is it that one misses?

Perhaps a home to which there is no return.

An actual conversation need not give that longing somewhere to rest. Silence face to face does not always bring a flood of tears. More often the silence simply catches in the throat.

Not necessarily because both sides have much to say and feeling will not let it through. Perhaps neither wishes to speak; perhaps both are keeping away from the same place.

In the conversation I reached for “the elephant in the room,” then found it wrong. This was not a great solid presence, not something that could at last be acknowledged and pointed out so that we might begin to deal with it. More a hollow, a black hole, even. We know it is there and keep our distance, lest we be drawn in.

Whatever we speak of, the words seem to stop short.

The difficulty goes further. Were we to stop avoiding it and speak directly of the hollow, the words might stop short all the same. It is not a matter of choosing the right subject and finally arriving. Speak of something else, and it seems we have not reached it; speak of it, and we know we have not reached it either.

There is no easy courage here with which to promise that speaking will make things better.

I began to speak because I could not write. The conversation traveled from writing to this point without finding an account in which everything could rest. Some things have become clearer in the telling, have genuinely touched me; elsewhere, ceasing to avert my eyes has brought no greater clarity.

There is still, in some measure, that catch in the throat. Still no words.
